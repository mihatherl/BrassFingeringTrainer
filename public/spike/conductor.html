Nothing flashes on the beat. No colour change, no pulse, and the beat number is off by default — every one of those would give the beat away by some means other than the movement, which is the only thing being tested. If the ictus does not read from a dot it will not read from a drawn conductor either.

Two questions. First: can you find the beat at all, with the metronome off? Turn it on afterwards to check whether where you thought the beat was is where it actually is. Second, and the doubtful one: drag the tempo while playing. That is a rit., and it is the thing a metronome can never teach. If you cannot follow it here, the conductor is a nice touch for steady playing and no more.

The Arcs row is the shape half of the style axis, which did not exist until recently. The axis has always been described as little rebound for a lyrical phrase against a sharp ictus for a march — timing and shape — but only the timing was ever built. With the checkbox off, the drawn curve at smooth and at marcato is not merely similar, it is identical: the only difference is that the hand pauses longer, and at both ends the slowest frame moves under a pixel. There was nothing to see. Tick it and the style sets the shape as well.

The second slider on that row is the downbeat, and it is on its own curve deliberately. Scaled with the other arcs it fails twice over: the downbeat vanishes at the smooth end, where a legato phrase still needs a recognisable one, and it grows past any length an arm could hold a baton at when the arcs go up. At 0 the downbeat holds its height whatever the arcs do; at 100 it is just another arc, which is what it used to be. Smoothing also brings the beats themselves closer together now — every pattern but the four has two ictus points at different heights, and leaving that gap fixed left a bounce in the gesture that the style setting was supposed to have taken out.

The compound question, which is what the two extra sliders are for. 6/8 is beaten in two, 9/8 in three, 12/8 in four — the same shapes as 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4, so nothing new is drawn. What the reference says differs is the motion: compound time "carries more bounce, or air, between each beat", and the arrival point should be emphasised more than the travel between gestures. Both sliders start at no difference, and staying there is a real answer. Lag is the timing reading — the hand lingers longer between beats and snaps harder through them. Lift is the geometric one — the arcs simply rise higher. Set a compound metre and flip between it and its simple twin at the same tempo and style; the metronome ticks the three subdivisions under the pulse, quietly, so the compound feel is actually there to be judged against.

These are drawn as the band sees them. Every conducting diagram is from the conductor's own side, so beat two of a four pattern goes to their left — which arrives on your right. The patterns here are mirrored accordingly.

The three shapes share one moving point: the tip. Nothing about any of them is a recording — every position is worked out from the beat, which is the only reason a rit. can be followed at all.

The baton is one shape drawn twice. The grip travels the same pattern as the tip, smaller and concentric inside it, and the slider is how much smaller.

The drawn line changes length, and it should. A baton is rigid in three dimensions and this is its shadow: pointed towards you it foreshortens to almost nothing, swept across the body it shows its full length. An earlier version held the line to one length on the grounds that a stretching stick looks wrong, which forced a three-dimensional gesture into a constraint it never had.

Every beat lands on the same floor, and the four strokes are not alike. One to two is a low round lobe out to the side; two to three is the long stroke right across the pattern, arcing over the top; three to four is the matching lobe coming back; four to one goes straight up and straight back down. The figure of eight is where that long stroke passes through the vertical, and the lobes are what hang either side of it — give every stroke the same arc and there is no eight, only a row of humps. The last stroke is also how a piece begins: baton held up, swiped down onto beat one. The rebound slider is how high those arcs go, and it remains the legato-to-marcato axis: shallow is a lyrical phrase, deep is a march. Try the smooth end — it should be harder to follow.

The two extent sliders are the dynamic. A conductor asking for something very quiet beats the same shape much smaller, and the two directions shrink by different amounts — a broad quiet phrase stays wide and goes shallow. The figure beside them is the weakest beat in the bar, since a pattern is only as readable as the beat you cannot find.
