The cycle. A short phrase, and a fermata on the last beat of its last bar. The hand beats normally, gives the held note its ictus, and stops beating — the sustain is a meld, drifting toward where the release will fall from, with the orb building violet through it. Then the release: a tiny lift — the breath — and the drop to the contact line. Your job is to come in on the drop. Play the downbeat (or tap the stage) and the page measures how far off the ictus you landed.

The prep is in the new tempo, by rule, and it is not a slider. One pulse of the resumed tempo; two when the tempo changes at the hold — the extra beat that establishes the new speed, per Robertson. With "new tempo" ticked, each hold releases into a different speed drawn from the same palette the app uses, so the drop also tells you how fast.

Hold lengths are random inside the range, so counting cannot substitute for watching. The metronome falls silent through the hold — no beats pass, so there is nothing to click — and its next click lands exactly on the re-entry, which is how you check the drop with your ears after trusting it with your eyes.

The sound stops at the lift. The held note sustains through the meld and comes off as the breath begins — that silence is itself a cue, and it is the rule the app will follow. With the microphone on, use headphones or turn the metronome and held note off, or the speaker will trigger the onset detector before your instrument does.

The figure that decides. The headline is the spread of your re-entries in milliseconds. If it lands tight — comparable to your spread against a plain click — the gesture works and the app builds it. If it will not come down whatever the sliders say, fermatas stay drawn and unscheduled until the gesture is right.
